MODULE 01

Storage Engines

B-trees, LSM trees, and the most important fork in the road of database design

You're at a whiteboard. The interviewer leans back, arms crossed, and says: "Why does Postgres use B-trees and Cassandra use LSM trees? Pick one. Tell me what would break if we swapped them."

Five seconds to start talking. What's the first sentence out of your mouth?

If you said "Well, it depends..." — that's the answer that gets you marked junior. By the end of this module, you'll have a much better one.

Meet the cast

Throughout this book you'll bump into two people. They'll argue. They'll be wrong sometimes. They'll teach you more by getting things wrong than by reciting definitions.

Maya

Senior staff engineer

12 years building OLTP systems at scale. Has seen Postgres clusters fall over in seventeen distinct ways. Drinks coffee black. Believes "it depends" is the laziest answer in distributed systems.

Sam

Mid-level engineer prepping for senior interviews

6 years of CRUD apps. Knows Postgres well enough to be dangerous. Just got reading list of "things you should already know" and is panicking. Sam asks the questions you wish you could ask without judgment.

Sam is interviewing in three weeks. Maya has agreed to coach. They sat down with coffee. Here's how it begins.

Sam OK so I keep reading "B-tree vs LSM tree" everywhere. I get the words. But honestly? If you put me on a whiteboard right now, I'd draw a tree and say "it has nodes." Then I'd look at you with hopeful eyes.

Maya That's exactly what most candidates do. And it's why most candidates don't pass. Storage engines aren't trivia. They're the most concrete tradeoff in databases. Every other concept — replication, indexes, consistency — sits on top of the choice the database made down here.

Sam Down where?

Maya Down at the disk. The actual bytes. Where do they go when you write them? In what order do they sit? And — this is the question that decides everything — when do you do the work of sorting them?

Sam Sorting? Why does sorting matter?

Maya Because reads are fast on sorted data and slow on unsorted data. Period. That's the whole game. Now: do you sort now (every time you write) or later (in a background batch)? Pick one. It changes everything.

Stop. Read that last paragraph again. That's the entire chapter in one sentence.

The fork in the road

There are two answers to "when do you do the sorting?" Just two. They define the entire history of database storage engines.

Sort on write = B-trees. Pay the cost up front, every time. Fast reads, slower writes.

Sort later = LSM trees. Pay the cost in the background. Fast writes, more complicated reads.

That's it. That's the dichotomy. Everything else in this chapter is consequences.

But "consequences" matter. Consequences are what gets asked about in interviews. Consequences are what bites you at 3 AM. Let's earn the consequences.

A tale of two writes

Let's make this concrete with two systems doing the exact same thing.

The setup: identical hardware. NVMe SSD. 32 GB RAM. One CPU. Two databases:

  • System A: PostgreSQL (B-tree storage engine for tables and indexes).
  • System B: Cassandra (LSM tree storage engine).

Same schema. We'll write 10 million rows in a tight loop and then query a single row.

What happens?

You'd probably guess that the more "modern" system (Cassandra) is faster at everything. Or that they're roughly comparable because — hey — same hardware.

Cassandra ingests writes about 5-10x faster than Postgres. Postgres reads the resulting data about 2-3x faster for point lookups. They're not "comparable." They're optimized for opposite things.

How can the same hardware produce wildly different results? Because the hardware loves some I/O patterns and hates others. The two engines play to different strengths.

Why the disk has feelings

Before we go further, you need to internalize one fact about every storage device that has ever existed.

Sequential I/O is wildly faster than random I/O. On HDDs, ~100x. On SATA SSDs, ~10x. On NVMe SSDs, still ~3-5x. Even on RAM, sequential reads beat random reads because of CPU caching.

Imagine a librarian. She works in a library where every book is on a shelf in alphabetical order.

"Bring me ten books in a row from the same shelf." Easy. She walks to the shelf, grabs them, walks back. Done in 30 seconds.

"Bring me ten random books from anywhere in the library." She walks to one shelf. Grabs it. Walks across the building. Grabs another. Walks to a third floor. And so on. 10 minutes.

Same ten books. Same librarian. 30 seconds vs 10 minutes. That's sequential vs random I/O.

The disk isn't fundamentally different from this librarian. It hates random work, even with SSDs. SSDs moderate the pain compared to spinning disks, but they don't eliminate it.

This is why sorting matters. Sorted data on disk = sequential reads = fast. Unsorted data = random reads = slow.

So now the question becomes: how do you keep data sorted despite the fact that real workloads insert keys in random order?

The two engines give different answers.

B-trees: sort as you go

A B-tree is exactly what it sounds like: a tree, but with fat nodes. Each node holds many keys (often hundreds). It's also balanced — every leaf is the same depth from the root.

                        ┌─────────────────────┐
                        │  [50, 100, 200]      │   ← root (in memory)
                        └──┬──────┬──────┬─────┘
                           │      │      │
          ┌────────────────┘      │      └────────────────┐
          ▼                       ▼                       ▼
    ┌──────────┐           ┌──────────┐           ┌──────────┐
    │ [10, 30] │           │ [60, 80] │           │ [120,150]│   ← internal
    └──┬───┬───┘           └──┬───┬───┘           └──┬───┬───┘
       │   │                  │   │                  │   │
       ▼   ▼                  ▼   ▼                  ▼   ▼
    leaves with actual rows of data

A B-tree of branching factor 3. Real ones have branching factors of 100-300.

To find a key, you walk from the root to a leaf. Compare the key against the root's separators, pick a child, recurse. Done in 3-4 steps even for huge databases.

To find row with key=125 in the tree above:

Load root: [50, 100, 200]. 125 is between 100 and 200, so go to the rightmost child.

Load that child: [120, 150]. 125 is between 120 and 150, so go to the middle child.

Load the leaf. The row is there. Return it.

Three page reads. No matter how big the database. A 10 GB B-tree and a 10 TB B-tree both take ~4 reads, because adding data just adds depth to the tree, and depth grows logarithmically.

So far so good for reads. Now let's see what happens on a write.

What a B-tree does on every single write

Inserting a new row with key=42 into a B-tree:

Walk the tree to find which leaf page should contain key 42. (~3-4 page reads. Mostly cached, but always at least one.)

Load that leaf page from disk into memory.

Insert the new key in the right sorted position within the page. This may require shifting other keys around inside the page.

Write the entire page back to disk. Yes — even if you only changed one byte, the disk write is for the whole 8 KB page.

Did the leaf overflow? Then split it into two pages, update the parent. Did the parent now overflow? Split it too. In the worst case, splits cascade up to the root.

Note step 4. Every write is a random page write. You picked some leaf out of millions; the next write picks a different one. The disk hates this, remember?

This is where most "Postgres write performance" complaints come from — random page writes plus the WAL fsync (next module).

Postgres mitigates this with the buffer pool (a giant in-memory cache of pages) and lazy flushing (write to disk during checkpoints, not immediately). But fundamentally: B-tree writes are random page writes.

LSM trees: sort later, much later

The LSM tree (Log-Structured Merge tree) takes a radically different approach: never touch the existing data on disk. Just keep appending.

That sounds insane. But once you see it, you'll understand why every modern write-heavy database uses some flavor of LSM.

Write path:

write request │ ▼ ┌──────────────────┐ ┌────────────────────┐ │ in-memory │ │ commit log │ │ sorted memtable │ ──and── │ append-only file │ ← protects durability │ (skip list) │ │ on disk │ └──────────────────┘ └────────────────────┘ │ memtable fills up... │ ▼ ┌──────────────────┐ │ flushed to disk │ │ as immutable │ SSTable_1 │ SSTable file │ └──────────────────┘

Many writes later...

┌──────────────┐ ┌──────────────┐ ┌──────────────┐ │ SSTable_1 │ │ SSTable_2 │ │ SSTable_3 │ └──────┬───────┘ └──────┬───────┘ └──────┬───────┘ │ │ │ └─────────┬───────┴────────────────┘ ▼ COMPACTION (background) ▼ ┌──────────────────┐ │ SSTable_merged │ single sorted file └──────────────────┘

Let's walk through one write to see the magic.

Inserting a new row with key=42 into an LSM tree:

Append the change to a commit log on disk. (Sequential write — fast.)

Insert the row into an in-memory sorted structure called a memtable. (Pure RAM operation — extremely fast.)

Acknowledge the write. The client thinks it's done. It is done, in any meaningful sense.

Eventually, when the memtable gets big (say, 64 MB), the database flushes it to disk as a brand-new file called an SSTable ("Sorted String Table"). Sequential write. Once written, the SSTable is never modified.

Over time, you accumulate many SSTables — all sorted internally, but separate from each other. A background process called compaction merges them into bigger sorted files.

Notice what's missing. There's no random page rewrite. No tree mutation. No splits. Every disk operation is sequential. The disk is happy.

Read step 3 again. The database acked the write with the data only in RAM and a commit log. Why is that safe? Because the commit log is on disk. If the server crashes, on restart, it replays the commit log and rebuilds the memtable. No data lost.

This is exactly the same trick a B-tree uses with its WAL — we'll cover that in Module 02. The LSM just leans on it harder.

Same insight, three different ways

Sometimes the same idea clicks differently for different people. Here's the LSM tree explained in three ways. Find the one that sticks.

Way 1: a desk metaphor

You're an analyst. People bring you papers all day. You're supposed to keep them sorted by name.

Strategy A (B-tree): Every time someone hands you a paper, you walk to the filing cabinet, find the right alphabetical position, and slot it in. If a folder gets too thick, you split it into two folders. You're always sorting.

Strategy B (LSM): People keep handing you papers. You toss each one into the inbox tray on your desk. The tray stays sorted (you keep it that way as you toss — easy with a small tray). When the tray overflows, you grab the whole stack, walk to the cabinet, and stuff it into a new folder labeled "today's batch." You don't merge it with existing folders. You just add a new folder.

At the end of each week, you take all the daily folders and merge them into one big "this week" folder, throwing away duplicates. That's compaction.

Reading is harder under strategy B. When someone asks "do we have a paper for Alice?" you have to check the inbox tray, then today's folder, then yesterday's, then last week's. That's why LSM trees get fancy with Bloom filters (next module).

Way 2: a money metaphor

B-trees are like paying off your credit card every day. Small, steady, predictable. You never accumulate debt, but you also can't spend in bursts.

LSM trees are like deferring your bills until the end of the month. Cheap moment-to-moment, big bill at the end. You can spend in bursts.

The "bills" here are I/O operations. Compaction is the bill arriving.

Way 3: ascii animation

Watch a sequence of writes to the same LSM tree.

TIME 0: memtable: [empty] disk: (no SSTables yet)

TIME 1: write key=50, key=20, key=80 memtable: [20, 50, 80] disk: (no SSTables yet)

TIME 2: memtable fills up memtable: [empty] disk: SSTable_001 = [20, 50, 80]

TIME 3: write key=30, key=70 memtable: [30, 70] disk: SSTable_001 = [20, 50, 80]

TIME 4: memtable fills again memtable: [empty] disk: SSTable_001 = [20, 50, 80] SSTable_002 = [30, 70]

TIME 5: write UPDATE key=50 → new value memtable: [50'] ← the prime mark means "newer version" disk: SSTable_001 = [20, 50, 80] SSTable_002 = [30, 70]

       ↑

THE BIG INSIGHT: SSTable_001 still contains the OLD value for key=50. The LSM tree never touches it. The memtable has the new value. On read, you find the new value first. The old one becomes obsolete and will be cleaned up during compaction.

TIME 6: compaction merges SSTable_001 and SSTable_002 memtable: [50'] disk: SSTable_merged = [20, 30, 70, 80] ↑ Note: key=50 is gone! Why? Because the merger saw that key=50 in memtable was newer than key=50 in SSTable_001, and 50 wasn't in SSTable_002. So the merger dropped the obsolete one.

Once you see how the layers work, the rest of the LSM design follows.

The brutal tradeoff

Now let's put them side by side and really compare.

B-tree

  • Random writes — every insert/update touches a leaf somewhere in the tree.
  • Read-modify-write per page on every change. Even one byte = whole 8KB page.
  • One place per key — no duplicates on disk.
  • Fast point reads — log(N) page traversals, all cached after warmup.
  • Range scans excellent — leaves often linked, just walk them.
  • Write amplification: ~1-2x. Page rewrites cost you maybe double the data volume in actual disk writes.
  • Updates: cheap. Just mutate in place.
  • Deletes: cheap. Mark the slot free.

Cost paid: at write time.

LSM tree

  • Sequential writes only — append to memtable in RAM, flush to new SSTable on disk.
  • Append-only on disk — files never modified once written.
  • Same key may exist in many SSTables with different versions.
  • Slower point reads — must check memtable + multiple SSTables.
  • Range scans need merge across SSTables.
  • Write amplification: 10-30x. Every byte gets rewritten multiple times during compaction.
  • Updates: implemented as new write; old version dies during compaction.
  • Deletes: write a "tombstone" marker; cleanup during compaction.

Cost paid: at compaction time.

Hold on. The LSM tree writes more data total — sometimes 30x more — and yet it's faster on writes? That's not a typo?

(Think about this for 30 seconds before reading the answer.)

...

The answer is in two words: sequential and batched.

Sequential writes are 5-100x faster than random writes (depending on storage type). So even if the LSM writes 10x more total bytes, it does so sequentially — and that's still faster than the B-tree's random page rewrites.

Plus, compaction runs in the background, asynchronously. The user-facing write path doesn't pay that cost in latency. The disk pays in bandwidth, but you have lots of bandwidth — you don't have lots of latency.

Read paths compared in detail

This is where most candidates trip up. The read paths are different, and the difference is the source of every interesting LSM optimization.

B-tree read path

Reading user_id=12345 from a Postgres B-tree:

Load the root page (almost certainly in the buffer pool — it's the most-accessed page).

Binary search the root to find the right child pointer.

Load the child page. Likely cached if it's a hot tree.

Binary search again. Find the right child.

Load the leaf. Cached or not depending on workload.

Find the row. If it's a clustered index (MySQL InnoDB), the row data IS the leaf. If it's a secondary index (Postgres), follow the pointer to the heap to fetch the actual row.

Total: 3-4 page reads, plus possibly one heap fetch. ~10-50 microseconds for a hit.

LSM tree read path

Reading user_id=12345 from a Cassandra LSM tree:

Check the memtable. If the key is there with a recent version, return it. (Pure RAM, < 1 microsecond.)

If not in memtable, check the most recent SSTable. Each SSTable has a Bloom filter (Module 02!) that tells you "this key is definitely not in this SSTable" without doing any disk I/O. If the Bloom filter says "no," skip this SSTable.

For each SSTable whose Bloom filter says "maybe," seek into the SSTable's index, then into its data section. (Disk I/O — slow.)

If you find the key, return the most-recent version. (You might find older versions in older SSTables, but the first one wins because SSTables are checked newest-first.)

Total: depends entirely on how many SSTables you accumulated and your Bloom filter false positive rate. A well-tuned LSM with leveled compaction: ~1-2 disk reads. A poorly-tuned LSM under write storm: 10+ disk reads.

A senior candidate, when describing LSM reads, mentions Bloom filters in the same breath. It signals you understand the engineering tradeoff, not just the data structure. Practice this: never say "LSM reads are slower" without immediately following with "...but Bloom filters mitigate this."

Compaction: where the dragons live

Compaction is the LSM tree's secret heart and its most common operational pain.

Compaction merges multiple SSTables into fewer, larger ones — removing duplicates, applying tombstones, and reducing read amplification.

It runs in the background. It eats:

  • Disk I/O (rewriting SSTables takes a lot of bandwidth).
  • CPU (merge logic, decompression/compression).
  • SSD endurance (extra writes shorten the SSD's life).

When compaction can't keep up with writes, you get read amplification: more SSTables to check per query. Reads slow down. Latency spikes. The cluster wobbles.

The "Cassandra fell over under write storm" pattern.

A team I know was running Cassandra for time-series data. Things were fine for months. Then a new feature shipped and write volume tripled.

For about an hour, things were better than fine — Cassandra absorbed the writes effortlessly. Memtables filled, flushed, new SSTables piled up.

Then around 90 minutes in, p99 read latency suddenly went from 5ms to 800ms. The dashboards lit up red.

What happened? Compaction couldn't keep up. The number of SSTables per partition went from ~5 (steady state) to ~80. Every read was now checking 80 Bloom filters and (because their Bloom filter sizing was too aggressive — too many false positives) actually doing 5-10 disk seeks per read.

The fix wasn't more capacity. It was tuning the compaction strategy — switching from SizeTieredCompactionStrategy to LeveledCompactionStrategy, which keeps the number of SSTables per query bounded.

The lesson: understanding compaction is understanding LSM operations.

There are two main compaction strategies you should know by name.

Size-tiered compaction (STCS)

When you have N SSTables of similar size, merge them. The result is a bigger SSTable. Wait for N more similarly-sized to accumulate. Repeat.

Wins: cheap to maintain. Low write amplification.

Loses: number of SSTables per partition is unbounded. Reads can hit many SSTables.

Use when: write-heavy, append-mostly, query patterns can tolerate higher read amplification.

Leveled compaction (LCS)

SSTables are organized into levels. Level 0 has small recent SSTables. Level 1 is bigger and fully sorted across the level (no overlap between SSTables). Level 2 is bigger again. And so on.

Reads check at most one SSTable per level (because levels are non-overlapping). With ~7 levels, that's 7 SSTables max — bounded.

Wins: low read amplification (bounded SSTable checks).

Loses: high write amplification (each level rewrite costs a lot).

Use when: read-heavy, latency-sensitive workloads. Want predictable read performance.

Leveled is for reads. Size-tiered is for writes. Pick by what hurts more in your workload.

Sam tries to convince Maya

Sam OK wait. If LSM is this great for writes, why doesn't everyone use it? Why is Postgres still the default for new projects?

Maya Because Postgres is optimized for the most common workload, which is mixed reads and writes with frequent updates to existing rows. Think a SaaS app. Users update profiles. Orders update statuses. Counters increment. That's a B-tree's home turf.

LSM trees handle UPDATE poorly. They don't update — they write a new version. The old one sits around taking up disk until compaction. If you're updating the same rows over and over, your LSM tree gets fat and slow.

Sam But Cassandra updates rows...

Maya Yes. Painfully. That's why Cassandra is famously not a good fit for "let's update this row a lot" workloads. It shines for append-mostly — events, logs, time-series, immutable history. Workloads where rows are written once and rarely updated.

Sam What about deletes?

Maya Same problem, worse. To delete, the LSM writes a tombstone — a marker that says "this key is deleted." The tombstone has to propagate through every SSTable that contains the key, which only happens during compaction. Until then, reads have to keep checking those SSTables and saying "oh, that's deleted." If you delete a lot, you accumulate tombstones, and your reads get slower.

Cassandra deployments at scale routinely have to deal with the "tombstone problem." There's even a metric for it.

Sam So picking LSM over B-tree is basically saying "my workload is append-only or close to it"?

Maya That's a good summary. Append-mostly. Write-heavy. Tolerant of read-side complexity. Wants horizontal scale at low write cost. That's where LSM shines.

Three concrete decision scenarios

Now let's take the framework and apply it. Here are three workloads. For each, I'll give you the right answer and the reasoning — because the answer is less important than how you arrive at it.

Workload 1: A SaaS billing system.

  • 10K users, growing slowly.
  • Each user has a profile, subscriptions, invoices.
  • Reads vastly outnumber writes (~50:1).
  • Writes are mostly updates to existing rows (subscription changes, invoice statuses).
  • Latency must be sub-100ms even at p99.

Choose: B-tree (Postgres or MySQL).

Why: low write volume, lots of updates to existing rows, latency-sensitive. The B-tree's "pay on write, fast on read" profile is exactly the profile of this workload. LSM would be working hard to absorb writes you don't have, while paying with read complexity you can't tolerate.

Workload 2: An IoT metrics ingestion service.

  • 1 million sensors writing one reading every 30 seconds.
  • That's ~33K writes/sec, sustained.
  • Queries are mostly "give me the last hour for sensor X" or "give me averages for sensor group Y in the last day."
  • Storage cost matters — historical data must be cheap.

Choose: LSM tree (Cassandra, ScyllaDB, or InfluxDB-style).

Why: write-dominant. Append-only data (no updates to old readings). Range queries on recent data work great with time-windowed compaction (TWCS in Cassandra) — recent SSTables are small and few, so reads are cheap. The B-tree would melt under 33K random writes/sec; the LSM absorbs them with sequential I/O.

Workload 3: An online multiplayer game's player state.

  • 5 million daily active players.
  • Each player has a session state that updates several times per second.
  • Strict latency requirements (sub-50ms reads and writes).
  • Write rate: ~200K writes/sec at peak.

This one's a trap. Write rate is high (LSM territory), but the access pattern is update the same key constantly (B-tree territory). Pure LSM accumulates obsolete versions; pure B-tree can't keep up with random page writes at that rate.

Choose: a key-value store with hybrid characteristics, or partition the workload.

Real systems use Redis or Memcached as a hot in-memory cache (so most writes don't hit disk synchronously), with periodic checkpointing to durable storage. Some engines like RocksDB tune compaction to handle update-heavy workloads better.

The point: don't dogmatically pick one engine. Match the engine to the access pattern, not just the volume.

Sharpen your pencil

Time to actually do something. Don't skip these.

Exercise 1. A startup's logging service writes 50K events/sec. Each event is 200 bytes. Events are read by humans investigating bugs — usually "give me all events for trace_id X in the last hour."

You have to recommend a storage engine. Give your answer in 3 sentences: which engine, why, and what risk you're taking.

LSM tree (e.g., Cassandra with TWCS, or Elasticsearch's underlying Lucene which is LSM-shaped). Reasoning: 50K writes/sec is well into the territory where B-tree random I/O hurts, and events are append-only — never updated, rarely deleted. The risk: query patterns are "fetch by trace_id," which means partitioning by trace_id (so all related events live together) is critical; if I partition by timestamp, queries fan out across partitions and read amp explodes. I'd verify the access pattern before committing.

Exercise 2. Your team is debating whether to migrate a Postgres database to Cassandra. The reason given: "we need to scale." The current Postgres is at 30% CPU and 40% disk usage on a single node.

Should you migrate? Yes or no, plus your three follow-up questions before deciding.

No — at least not yet, and probably not ever for that workload. Three questions:

  1. What's the actual bottleneck? 30% CPU and 40% disk on a single node is nowhere near the limit. If reads are slow, the issue is missing indexes or query patterns, not storage engine. If writes are slow, why? Is it lock contention, WAL saturation, or actual write throughput?

  2. What's the workload shape? If it's update-heavy SaaS data, Cassandra is the wrong tool period. If it's append-mostly events that have grown beyond a single Postgres can hold, then yes — but you'd partition Postgres before reaching for Cassandra.

  3. Has anyone profiled the slow queries? Most "we need to scale" conversations are actually "we have one slow query that's locking up everything." Migrating engines doesn't fix that — it just gives the same query a new way to be slow on a less-familiar system.

The senior reflex: "scaling" is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Diagnose first.

Exercise 3. Explain in your own words, to a hypothetical Sam who doesn't know anything yet, why "LSM trees turn random writes into sequential writes" is a big deal. Two sentences.

Random writes hammer the disk because each write is a separate seek-and-write to a different location, and disks/SSDs are much slower at random I/O than sequential I/O. LSM trees absorb writes into a sorted in-memory structure first, then flush them to disk in big sequential chunks — so even though the database might write the same data multiple times during compaction, every actual disk operation is fast and sequential.

There are no dumb questions

Q: Why does Postgres still use B-trees in 2026 if LSMs are "better" at scale?

A: Because "better at scale" is half the picture. Postgres is optimized for the most common database workload — OLTP with mixed reads/writes, frequent updates to existing rows. B-trees handle UPDATE and DELETE elegantly. There's one place to mutate. LSMs handle them via tombstones, which create cleanup work and can hurt read performance. For analytical or write-heavy or time-series workloads, the calculus flips. Tools should match workloads.

Q: Are there hybrid engines?

A: Yes — and they're getting more common. SQL Server has "in-memory OLTP" with hybrid structures. MyRocks is RocksDB-on-MySQL (LSM under MySQL's wire protocol). Postgres has experimental zheap and various extensions like cstore_fdw for columnar tables. Modern engines blur the line. Tikv, the storage layer of TiDB and CockroachDB, is LSM-based but used for transactional workloads with careful tuning.

Q: What's a "B+ tree" — different from a B-tree?

A: Yes, slightly. A B+ tree is a B-tree where the leaves are linked together in a sorted list. This makes range scans much faster — once you find the start of a range, you just follow the leaf links. Almost every "B-tree" you'll encounter in a real database is actually a B+ tree. People say "B-tree" colloquially. In an interview, "B-tree" is fine; if pressed, mention you mean B+ tree.

Q: Why does the LSM tree need both a memtable and a commit log?

A: The memtable is in RAM — fast for reads and writes, but volatile. If the server crashes, the memtable is gone. The commit log is on disk — durable, but only useful for recovery, not for serving reads (you'd have to scan the whole log to find a key). They're two parts of the same design: commit log for durability, memtable for speed. On crash recovery, the database replays the commit log to rebuild the memtable.

Q: Is "LSM" the same as "log-structured filesystem"?

A: They share the same idea (sequential writes, occasional compaction) but are different things. Log-structured filesystems (LFS, F2FS) apply the principle to whole filesystem. LSM trees apply it to a key-value index. The original LSM paper is from 1996 and built on filesystem ideas.

Q: Can compaction run too aggressively?

A: Yes. If compaction runs too hot, it competes with foreground reads/writes for I/O and CPU. Cassandra has knobs to throttle compaction (compaction_throughput_mb_per_sec) — and tuning them is an art. Too low: SSTables pile up, reads slow down. Too high: foreground latency spikes from I/O contention. A senior operator monitors the SSTable count and the compaction lag and tunes accordingly.

Q: What about "fractal trees" — I saw that mentioned somewhere?

A: That's TokuDB's storage engine — a B-tree variant that buffers writes inside the internal nodes before propagating them down. It's effectively a B-tree/LSM hybrid: the buffering gives some of the LSM's write performance while keeping B-tree-style structure. Cool idea but TokuDB itself didn't dominate the market. The technique survives in research and in some specialized engines.

Q: How do I tell which engine my database uses?

A: For most major databases, just know it: Postgres = B-tree, MySQL InnoDB = B-tree, Mongo (WiredTiger) = B-tree by default, Cassandra = LSM, Scylla = LSM, RocksDB = LSM, LevelDB = LSM, HBase = LSM, BigTable = LSM, DynamoDB = LSM-flavored. SQL Server is B-tree (with columnstore as a separate option). Oracle is B-tree.

Three myths to dynamite before your interview

"LSM trees are always faster than B-trees."

False. LSM trees are faster on writes, especially under high write throughput, if the workload is append-mostly. For mixed read/write workloads with updates to existing rows, B-trees often beat LSMs on overall throughput and especially on tail latency.

"Bloom filters never give wrong answers."

False. Bloom filters give false positives. They never give false negatives. The whole point of the data structure is that asymmetry — "definitely not present" is reliable, "probably present" is a guess.

"WAL is slow because it's a log."

False. WAL is fast because it's append-only sequential I/O. What's slow is fsync() — the syscall that forces data to physically reach the disk. The performance question is "how often do we fsync?" not "do we use a WAL?" We'll cover this fully in Module 02.

Interview gotchas

These are the specific traps senior interviewers love. Memorize them.

The "everything is a tradeoff" trap. If the interviewer asks "B-tree or LSM?" and you answer "it depends," they will press. Always commit to a choice based on the workload they describe. "It depends" without follow-up signals junior. The senior version: "It depends on workload — for the workload you described (read-heavy, mixed updates), I'd pick B-tree, and here's why..."

Confusing LSM with append-only logs. LSMs are not just write-ahead logs or Kafka segments. They have sorted in-memory structures, multiple sorted files on disk, and compaction. A pure append-only log has no compaction-into-sorted-runs and no point-lookup capability without a separate index.

Forgetting Bloom filters when describing LSM read paths. Whenever you mention LSM reads, mention Bloom filters in the same breath. It signals depth.

Saying "the WAL is for replication." WAL is for durability (local crash recovery). It overlaps with replication in some systems (Postgres physical replication ships the WAL to replicas), but conceptually it's a different abstraction. Replication uses the WAL; the WAL exists for recovery.

Not knowing that Postgres has heap files separate from indexes. In Postgres, an index is a B-tree of (key, heap_pointer). The actual row lives in the heap file. So an index lookup costs B-tree traversal plus a heap fetch. This is why covering indexes (Module 04) matter so much. In MySQL InnoDB, the primary key index is the data — leaves contain the rows directly. Different model.

Mock interview — the full transcript

Here's a real-feel interview Q&A. Watch how a senior candidate moves.

Walk me through what happens, end to end, when I write a single row to a Cassandra table.

Sure. Let me set the scene first — Cassandra is an LSM-based, leaderless distributed database, so a "write" actually involves both a coordinator-replica protocol and the local LSM machinery.

Sets context immediately. Doesn't dive into the data structure without framing.

At the cluster level: the client sends the write to any node, which becomes the coordinator for this write. The coordinator looks at the partition key and uses consistent hashing to figure out which replicas own this data — typically 3 replicas, configurable via replication factor.

The coordinator forwards the write to each of those replicas. On each replica, the write goes into two places in parallel: the commit log (an append-only WAL on disk for durability) and the memtable (an in-memory sorted structure).

Each replica acks back to the coordinator. The coordinator waits until enough replicas have acknowledged to satisfy the consistency level — for example, QUORUM with replication factor 3 means waiting for 2 acks. Once that threshold is met, the coordinator acks the client. So at the moment the client sees success, the data is durable on disk in at least 2 nodes' commit logs.

Distinguishes cluster-level from local. Mentions consistency level — shows awareness of the configuration surface.

On the local LSM side: the memtable accumulates writes in memory. When it fills up — by default ~64 MB or after a time threshold — it's flushed to disk as an immutable SSTable. From that point, reads can find the data either in the still-active memtable or in any of the SSTables.

Over time, SSTables accumulate. A background process called compaction merges them — combining smaller SSTables into bigger ones, applying tombstones, and removing obsolete versions. The compaction strategy is important: SizeTiered for write-heavy, Leveled for read-heavy, TimeWindow for time-series. Cassandra defaults to SizeTiered which is fine for most cases but produces high read amplification under heavy load.

Names compaction strategies. Hints at operational reality. Senior signal.

The thing I'd flag for a senior conversation: at scale, the operational pain points are tombstone accumulation under heavy delete patterns, repair windows for hinted handoff and read repair, and compaction lag during write storms. Those are the things that wake people up at night, not the basic write path.

Drops in operational concerns proactively. Shows experience, not just knowledge.

What's the failure mode if the coordinator dies after acking the client but before the write is fully replicated?

Good question. Cassandra has a few mechanisms for this. First, the consistency level itself protects you — at QUORUM, the data was already on a majority of replicas before the ack. So even if the coordinator dies, the write survives.

If the consistency level was ONE — ack on one replica — and that replica also dies before gossip propagates to others, you can lose the write. That's the durability tradeoff of low consistency levels.

For replicas that should have received the write but didn't (because they were down or the network blipped), Cassandra uses hinted handoff: the coordinator stores a hint and replays it to the missing replica when it comes back. There's a hint window — by default 3 hours — after which hints expire, and you'd need a repair to restore consistency.

Anticipates the next layer. Knows the named mechanisms (hinted handoff, repair) and the time bounds. This is the difference between someone who memorized concepts and someone who's debugged Cassandra.

Last question: if you had to design a logging system from scratch and had to choose between Cassandra and Postgres, which would you pick and why?

For pure append-only log ingestion at high volume — Cassandra. The write profile matches the LSM strengths exactly. Time-windowed compaction handles the time-series shape well, and partitioning by service or trace_id distributes load.

But — and this matters — for most logging use cases at most companies, I'd actually pick a purpose-built tool: Elasticsearch or Loki for search-friendly logs, or a managed service. Cassandra is great when you genuinely need TBs/sec of writes and full control. Below that scale, the operational overhead of running Cassandra outweighs the benefit.

Postgres for logs is fine up to maybe a few thousand writes per second on a beefy node. Past that, you're fighting against the storage engine.

Doesn't just answer the question — broadens it to "what's the right tool for the actual scale?" Mentions managed services. Signals that the candidate thinks about TCO, not just throughput.

Cheat sheet

Storage Engines — One Page

B-tree (Postgres, MySQL InnoDB, SQLite, Mongo WiredTiger)

  • Sorted, balanced, in-place mutation.
  • Random I/O on writes; cached for reads.
  • Excellent for: read-heavy, mixed reads/writes, frequent updates, range scans, latency-sensitive.
  • Pain point: random write throughput ceiling.

LSM tree (Cassandra, RocksDB, ScyllaDB, LevelDB, HBase, BigTable, DynamoDB-style)

  • Memtable + commit log + immutable SSTables + compaction.
  • Sequential I/O on writes; multi-file lookups on reads.
  • Excellent for: write-heavy, append-mostly, time-series, very large datasets.
  • Pain points: tombstone accumulation under heavy deletes, compaction lag under sustained write storms, read amplification when SSTable count grows.

The 30-second verbal answer

"B-trees write in place — they pay for sorting on every write, which makes reads simple but caps random write throughput. LSMs append to immutable sorted files in batch and merge them in the background, which makes writes fast but reads more complex. Pick by workload: read-heavy with updates → B-tree. Write-heavy and append-mostly → LSM."

Always pair these together when speaking

  • LSM tree → memtable, commit log, SSTable, compaction, Bloom filter, write amplification.
  • B-tree → buffer pool, page split, fill factor, WAL, fsync.
  • Both → sequential vs random I/O, read amplification, write amplification.

Compaction strategies (LSM only)

  • Size-tiered (STCS): low write amp, high read amp. Default in Cassandra. Good for write-heavy.
  • Leveled (LCS): low read amp, higher write amp. Best for read-latency-sensitive workloads.
  • Time-windowed (TWCS): best for time-series; per-window SSTables let old data compact away cleanly.

What to mention if asked about real-world LSM operations

  • Tombstone problem under delete-heavy patterns.
  • Compaction throughput tuning.
  • Bloom filter false positive rate trade-off (size vs CPU).
  • Repair, hinted handoff (for distributed LSMs like Cassandra).

Pick by workload, not by hype. The right answer depends on what you're writing, how often, and how it's read.
