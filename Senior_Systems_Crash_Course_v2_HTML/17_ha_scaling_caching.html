MODULE 17

High Availability, Scaling, Caching & Backpressure

How systems gracefully degrade under load instead of cascading into total outage

Your downstream service slows down. Latency creeps up. Your service starts queueing requests instead of failing fast. The queue fills. Memory pressure rises. The OOM killer fires. Your service crashes. The next service in line, which was depending on yours, follows it down. And down.

This is a cascading failure. They turn one slow service into a total outage.

This module is about the patterns that prevent them — and the operational discipline that distinguishes systems that survive load from systems that don't.

Sam survives his first incident

Sam OK we had our first major outage last week. The recommendation service got slow. Within 20 minutes, our entire checkout flow was down. The catalog service crashed. The user service started timing out. Then everything was on fire.

Maya Welcome to cascading failures. It's the most common form of distributed system outage. One service degrades, others depending on it pile on retries, the slow service gets slower, more retries pile on, and the whole thing collapses.

Sam We had retries! That's good, right?

Maya Retries are exactly the problem. When a service is slow, retries amplify the load. The slow service gets more requests, gets slower, triggers more retries. Without backpressure and circuit breakers, retries are how you turn one slow service into a total system outage.

Sam So how do you actually prevent this?

Maya Multiple layers. Backpressure to slow down upstream callers when you can't keep up. Circuit breakers to stop calling a degraded service entirely. Bulkheads to isolate failures so they don't spread. Caching to shed read load. Graceful degradation so the system survives in a reduced state instead of collapsing.

By the end of this module you'll know all of these by name and be able to articulate when each applies. They're the operational discipline that separates senior engineers from junior ones.

HA isn't a single feature — it's a discipline of designing systems that gracefully degrade under load instead of cascading into total outage. The patterns: backpressure to slow upstream callers, circuit breakers to stop calling broken services, bulkheads to isolate failures, caching to shed load, and graceful degradation to keep core paths working when subsystems fail.

What "high availability" actually means

Most engineers throw "high availability" around without quantifying it. Senior engineers know what the numbers mean.

Availability Annual downtime Daily downtime Workload
99% (two nines) 3.65 days 14 minutes Personal websites, internal tools
99.9% (three nines) 8.76 hours 86 seconds Most B2C web apps
99.95% 4.38 hours 43 seconds Premium SaaS
99.99% (four nines) 52 minutes 8.6 seconds Critical SaaS, payment gateways
99.999% (five nines) 5.26 minutes 864 ms Telco infrastructure, financial trading
99.9999% (six nines) 31.5 seconds/year 86 ms Aerospace, life-critical

Each additional nine costs roughly 10x more. Going from 99.9% to 99.99% isn't a configuration change — it's an architectural one (multi-region active-active, automated failover, comprehensive observability, on-call rotation). Most teams claim five nines aspirationally but operate at three.

Senior reflex: when someone says "we need five nines," verify they understand the cost. Most apps don't actually need it. The right discipline is to match availability to business need, not to claim a number.

The math behind dependent services:

If service A depends on services B and C, the combined availability is the product: - A.availability = B × C - 0.999 × 0.999 = 0.998001 (about 17 hours/year of downtime)

If service A has 10 dependencies each at 99.9%: - 0.999^10 = 0.99004 (88 hours/year — under 99% combined)

The senior insight: in a microservice architecture with many dependencies, individual service availability targets must exceed the desired system availability. If you want 99.9% system availability, your individual services likely need 99.99% — each. Or your architecture must tolerate dependency failures gracefully.

This is why graceful degradation matters more than chasing nines. You can't make every dependency perfectly available; you can make your service work when they're not.

Backpressure: the foundational pattern

The single most important pattern for preventing cascading failures.

Backpressure is the discipline of slowing down upstream callers when downstream can't keep up. Instead of accepting work you can't process, you signal "I'm full, please slow down" — letting the upstream caller queue, drop, or apply its own backpressure.

The cascading failure without backpressure:

Service A calls Service B. B normally responds in 50ms. Under load, B starts taking 5 seconds.

A's connection pool to B fills with pending requests. Each thread in A blocks waiting for B. A's request handlers can't free up. A starts timing out its own callers.

Without backpressure, A keeps accepting new requests, queueing them while threads block on B. Memory grows. The OOM killer eventually fires. A crashes.

Service C, which depends on A, starts seeing the same pattern. Cascading.

The same situation with backpressure:

Service A has a bounded queue for outgoing requests to B. When the queue is full, A rejects new incoming requests immediately with a 503 (Service Unavailable) and a Retry-After header.

The upstream caller (let's say a user-facing service) receives the 503. It can: - Show the user "service is temporarily busy, try again in a moment." - Apply its own backpressure to its callers. - Failover to a degraded mode (cached data, simpler response).

A doesn't crash. The slow service B gets less load, can recover. The system slows down gracefully instead of falling over.

Backpressure is the difference between a slow service and a dead system. The first is recoverable; the second is your weekend.

Implementing backpressure

The backpressure mechanisms:

Bounded queues. Every queue between components has a maximum size. When full, callers get a fast-fail signal.

Rate limiting. Cap incoming requests at a known-sustainable rate. Reject excess.

Connection pooling with bounded size. Limit how many simultaneous connections you'll make to downstream. Above that, queue or reject.

Token buckets / leaky buckets. Algorithmic rate limiting that smooths bursts.

Adaptive concurrency control. Tools like Netflix's concurrency-limits library that dynamically adjust based on observed latency.

Modern backpressure isn't just "fixed limits." It's adaptive — observing the system's actual response and adjusting. Netflix's concurrency-limits, AWS's adaptive retry, and Envoy's outlier detection all use this approach.

The principle: you don't need to know the right limit in advance. You need to detect when you're approaching it (rising latency, error rate, queue depth) and back off. Adaptive systems handle traffic shape changes that static limits can't.

Mention "adaptive concurrency control" in interviews about scaling patterns and you sound like someone who's read Brendan Burns or Netflix's tech blog.

Circuit breakers

A circuit breaker stops calling a service entirely once it's clearly broken.

A circuit breaker wraps calls to a downstream service. After N consecutive failures (or a failure rate threshold), it "opens" — subsequent calls fail immediately without even attempting the downstream service. After a timeout, it "half-opens" — lets one request through to test. Success closes the breaker; failure re-opens it.

Three states: closed (normal), open (failing fast), half-open (testing recovery).

                      CIRCUIT BREAKER STATE MACHINE

     ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
     │                                          │
     ▼                                          │

┌──────────────┐ │ │ CLOSED │ All calls pass through. │ │ (normal) │ Track success/failure rate. │ └──────┬───────┘ │ │ │ │ failure rate exceeds threshold │ ▼ │ ┌──────────────┐ │ │ OPEN │ Calls fail immediately. │ │ (failing │ Don't even try downstream. │ │ fast) │ │ └──────┬───────┘ │ │ │ │ wait period elapsed │ ▼ │ ┌──────────────┐ │ │ HALF-OPEN │ Let one call through. │ │ (testing) │ Success → close. │ │ │ Failure → re-open. │ └──────┬───────┘ │ │ │ │ success │ └──────────────────────────────────────────┘

Why circuit breakers help:

Without one: when a downstream service is down, every call to it times out (say, 30 seconds). Your service blocks threads waiting for these failed calls. Resources are consumed. Throughput drops to nothing.

With one: after a handful of failures, the circuit opens. Subsequent calls fail in microseconds — no waiting. Your service stays responsive, returns degraded data or appropriate error to its callers. Resources are preserved. You're failing fast instead of dying slowly.

When the downstream service recovers, the half-open state probes occasionally. Once it's responding, the breaker closes and normal traffic resumes.

Tools that implement circuit breakers:

  • Hystrix (Netflix, deprecated but historically dominant).
  • Resilience4j (the modern JVM successor).
  • Polly (.NET).
  • gobreaker (Go).
  • Envoy has built-in circuit breaker semantics for service mesh.
  • Istio wraps Envoy's capabilities.

For most modern Kubernetes-based architectures, the service mesh handles circuit breaking transparently — application code doesn't need to know.

Bulkheads: failure isolation

A bulkhead in a ship divides the hull into compartments. If one compartment floods, the ship doesn't sink — only that compartment is lost.

Bulkheading in software means isolating failure domains so a problem in one doesn't spread to others. Common implementations: separate thread pools per downstream, separate connection pools, separate processes, separate clusters.

Without bulkheads:

Your service has a single thread pool that handles all incoming requests. Each request might call User Service, Catalog Service, or Recommendation Service.

The Recommendation Service starts taking 30 seconds per call. Threads handling those requests block. Soon, all threads are blocked on Recommendation Service calls — even threads that just need to call User Service are queued behind them.

A degradation in one dependency took down all operations.

With bulkheads:

Your service has a separate thread pool for each downstream: - 50 threads for User Service. - 50 threads for Catalog Service. - 50 threads for Recommendation Service.

When Recommendation Service slows, only the recommendation thread pool fills. User Service requests still complete in 50ms. The system degrades partially — recommendations are unavailable, but core checkout works.

This is the graceful degradation mentioned earlier: lose a feature, keep the system running.

The Netflix architecture is famous for aggressive bulkheading. Each downstream call goes through its own connection pool, thread pool, and circuit breaker. When one of their hundreds of microservices fails, the impact is contained. Most users never notice.

Mention "Netflix-style bulkheading" in an interview about resilience and you signal real-world awareness.

Caching: the foundational performance pattern

We've touched on caching in modules about denormalization (Module 05) and CDC (Module 11). This is the comprehensive treatment.

The cache hierarchy

                        CACHING HIERARCHY

            Closest to user (fastest, smallest)
            ┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
            │  Browser cache (HTTP, IndexedDB)    │  ms
            ├─────────────────────────────────────┤
            │  CDN edge cache (Cloudflare, etc.)  │  ms
            ├─────────────────────────────────────┤
            │  Reverse proxy cache (Varnish, NG)  │  ms
            ├─────────────────────────────────────┤
            │  App-level cache (in-process)        │  μs
            ├─────────────────────────────────────┤
            │  Distributed cache (Redis, Memcached)│  ms (LAN)
            ├─────────────────────────────────────┤
            │  Database query cache                │  ms
            ├─────────────────────────────────────┤
            │  Database buffer pool                │  μs (RAM hit)
            ├─────────────────────────────────────┤
            │  Database disk                       │  ms (NVMe)
            └─────────────────────────────────────┘
                    Closest to data (slowest, largest)

Each cache layer is a chance to skip work. The senior framing: don't think about "where to cache?" — think about "which layers in this hierarchy are sized correctly?" A well-tuned system has caches at multiple layers, each serving a fraction of requests.

Cache strategies

The four classic patterns:

Cache-aside (lazy-load). App reads from cache. Miss → reads from DB, populates cache. Most common pattern. Simple. Risk: thundering herd if many concurrent misses.

Read-through. Cache itself fetches from DB on miss. Application doesn't see the DB directly. Common in CDN-style caches.

Write-through. App writes to cache; cache writes to DB. Stays consistent. Slower writes.

Write-behind (write-back). App writes to cache; cache asynchronously flushes to DB. Fast writes, risk of data loss if cache fails.

Cache-aside, the dominant pattern:

def get_user(user_id):
    # Try cache first
    user = cache.get(f"user:{user_id}")
    if user:
        return user

    # Cache miss — read from DB
    user = db.query("SELECT * FROM users WHERE id = ?", user_id)

    # Populate cache for next time
    cache.set(f"user:{user_id}", user, ttl=300)

    return user

The TTL ensures stale data eventually expires. The application owns the cache logic.

For invalidation on writes: app updates the DB, then deletes the cache entry (or updates it). Subsequent reads will re-populate.

Cache invalidation: the hard part

"There are only two hard things in computer science: cache invalidation and naming things." — Phil Karlton.

This is funny because it's true. Cache invalidation is genuinely hard.

Three failure modes:

  1. Stale cache after write: app writes to DB, doesn't invalidate cache. Next read returns old data. Common cause: developers forget the invalidation.

  2. Race conditions on invalidation: app deletes cache entry, but another request re-populates with the old DB value (because that request's read started before the write finished). Now the cache has stale data, and there's no signal to fix it until TTL.

  3. Distributed cache consistency: in a multi-region setup, cache invalidations might not reach all caches. Stale data persists in some regions.

The patterns to avoid invalidation pain:

  • Short TTLs. If data only needs to be "near-fresh," 5-minute TTL avoids most of these problems. Stale data corrects itself within 5 minutes.

  • Cache-on-write rather than invalidate-on-write. Update both DB and cache atomically (with idempotency to handle retries). Avoids the race conditions of invalidate-then-read.

  • CDC-driven cache updates. Module 11's pattern: changes to the DB flow through CDC to cache invalidation/update events. The cache stays in sync without application code coordinating.

  • Use cache versions in keys. user:42:v3 instead of user:42. Schema or logic changes bump the version; old keys age out via TTL.

The senior framing: the right cache pattern depends on consistency requirements. "Best-effort fresh" tolerates simple TTLs. "Must reflect recent writes" requires more careful invalidation or CDC pipelines.

The thundering herd problem

The cache stampede:

A popular cached entry expires. 1,000 simultaneous requests come in, all check the cache, all see a miss, all query the database simultaneously, all populate the cache.

For one entry, the database just took 1,000 unnecessary queries. Repeated across hundreds of expiring entries during a traffic spike, the cache layer becomes a load amplifier instead of a load reducer.

Three mitigations for thundering herd:

  • Probabilistic early expiration. Refresh entries just before they expire, with a small random offset. Spreads the refresh load.

  • Single-flight pattern. Only one request actually goes to the database for a given key; others wait for it to finish and use its result. Implemented via locks, semaphores, or libraries like Go's singleflight.

  • Stale-while-revalidate. Return the stale value immediately while asynchronously refreshing in the background. Users see no extra latency; the cache stays warm.

For high-traffic caches, at least one of these is essential. Without them, traffic spikes can melt your database.

Scaling strategies — the operational reality

By now we've covered the engineering primitives. Time to talk about scaling at the architectural level.

Vertical vs horizontal scaling

Vertical (scale up)

Bigger machine. More CPU, RAM, disk per node.

Pro: simple. No architectural change.

Con: hard ceiling — single-machine limits exist.

Pro: latency stays low (no network coordination).

Con: failure of single node = full outage.

Use first. Most teams go to horizontal too early.

Horizontal (scale out)

More machines. Distribute work across many nodes.

Pro: theoretically unlimited scale.

Con: coordination cost (Module 12).

Pro: failure tolerance through redundancy.

Con: operational complexity grows fast.

Use when vertical hits limits or HA requires it.

The senior progression:

  1. Single node with reasonable hardware. Most apps' first ~5 years.
  2. Vertical scale — bigger boxes for individual services that bottleneck.
  3. Read replicas for read scaling (Module 10).
  4. Caching layers to reduce database load.
  5. Horizontal partitioning when writes outgrow single node (Module 15).
  6. Multi-region when geographic distribution or DR demands.

Most teams skip steps 2-4 and jump to "we need microservices and Kubernetes." The right path is climbing the ladder one step at a time, validating that the simpler approach has been exhausted before adopting the next.

Multi-region active-active

The geographic HA scenario:

A company has US users and EU users. They want: - Local latency in each region (no cross-Atlantic round-trips). - Survival of an entire region failure. - Compliance with EU data residency rules.

The architecture: active-active multi-region. Both US and EU regions are live, accepting traffic. Data is replicated between them (async, with conflict resolution per Module 10).

For latency-critical reads/writes, traffic stays in the local region. For cross-region operations, traffic crosses with explicit awareness.

When a region fails (rare but real — see GitHub 2018), the other region absorbs traffic. Capacity must be provisioned to handle the full load on either side.

Multi-region active-active is expensive operationally. Conflict resolution must be explicit. Data residency complicates schemas. Cross-region partitions test the architecture's tolerance. Most teams that adopt it regret it within a year if they didn't truly need it.

Senior framing: only pursue multi-region active-active when (a) latency requirements demand local presence in multiple regions, OR (b) regulatory requirements force regional data isolation, OR (c) availability requirements exceed what single-region multi-AZ can deliver. Don't cargo-cult Netflix's architecture if you're a small SaaS.

Graceful degradation: the design philosophy

Bringing it all together, the unifying philosophy.

Graceful degradation is designing systems where the loss of any one component reduces functionality but doesn't stop the system. Each feature has a fallback path. Each call has a sane default. The system survives in a reduced state instead of cascading into total outage.

A graceful e-commerce checkout:

Normal: order created → payment charged → inventory decremented → shipping label printed → email sent.

When recommendation service is down: users still see products (no recommendations); checkout still works.

When personalization service is down: users see generic content; checkout still works.

When email service is down: order completes, email is queued for later delivery. User sees success.

When payment service is partially down: degrade to "we'll process when payment is available" with a saved order. Checkout doesn't fail; it queues.

When inventory service is unreliable: optimistic acceptance with eventual consistency. System trades occasional refunds for service-availability.

When the database is dead: everything is dead. The core dependency cannot be degraded around. This is what HA architecture must prevent.

The discipline of fallback paths:

Every external call should have a documented behavior for "what if this fails?" Most teams don't do this until incidents force them to. Senior teams do it during design.

A simple discipline: review every service call and ask "if this fails, what's the user experience?" The answers should be: - "User sees error" — sometimes correct (payment). - "User sees stale data" — often correct (catalog). - "User sees default" — sometimes correct (recommendations). - "User sees nothing" — usually wrong (the feature should be present even if degraded).

The framing: failures are not exceptions, they are expected events. Design for them.

Three more ways to think about resilience

Way 1: a hospital ER.

A hospital ER has triage. When patients overwhelm the system, the most critical cases get treated first; the less urgent wait. The hospital doesn't try to treat everyone simultaneously and collapse — it makes prioritization explicit.

That's backpressure. The ER says "we can handle 50 patients per hour; if 200 arrive, the rest wait or are redirected." Without it, the ER collapses and zero patients get treated.

A circuit breaker is "if a particular treatment isn't working today (say, an MRI machine is broken), the ER stops sending patients to MRI. It treats them with alternatives until the MRI is back online."

Bulkheads are separate care units. The cardiac unit's overflow doesn't take down the maternity ward.

Graceful degradation is the ER deciding "we can do core cases without elective procedures today."

Way 2: a busy restaurant.

A restaurant during a rush has natural backpressure: the host turns away walk-ins or quotes 90-minute waits. Without that, the kitchen would get overwhelmed and nobody would get fed.

If a single dish (say, the chef's special) takes 45 minutes due to a complex preparation, the restaurant might "86" it (remove from menu) — that's a circuit breaker. Other dishes still ship.

Bulkheads: separate stations for cold, hot, sauce, dessert. One station's slowness doesn't block the others.

Graceful degradation: when busy, the restaurant doesn't take "menu modifications." Standard dishes only. Speed up service by reducing flexibility.

Way 3: a city's water system.

When demand exceeds supply, the city doesn't fail — it institutes water restrictions. Lawn watering banned. Pool filling banned. Drinking water still flows. Critical uses preserved.

That's graceful degradation. The system isn't running at full capacity, but it's running. People notice the inconvenience but life continues.

Backpressure is the water pressure dropping when demand spikes — taps run slower instead of pipes bursting. The pressure system has self-protection.

Caching is rooftop water tanks. They serve the building when the main supply is busy. They smooth out demand spikes.

The whole architecture is designed for resilience under load, not just maximum throughput when everything's fine.

Sharpen your pencil

Exercise 1. A team's checkout service has 4 dependencies — user service, catalog service, payment service, and recommendation service. The recommendation service has been flaky for weeks. When it's slow, checkout slows. When it's down, checkout fails entirely. Architect a fix.

The diagnosis: tight coupling via shared infrastructure.

When recommendation service slows, the checkout service's threads block waiting for it. Eventually, no threads are available for any dependency call. Single dependency degradation has cascaded to total checkout failure.

The fix has three layers:

Layer 1: Bulkhead by dependency.

Separate connection pools and (if applicable) thread pools per downstream:

- user_service: pool of 50 connections
- catalog_service: pool of 50 connections
- payment_service: pool of 20 connections (the most critical, smaller pool to detect issues fast)
- recommendation_service: pool of 30 connections (least critical)

When recommendation calls slow, only the recommendation pool fills. User, catalog, and payment continue normally. Failure is contained.

Layer 2: Circuit breaker on recommendation.

Wrap calls to recommendation service in a circuit breaker. After 5 consecutive failures or 50% failure rate over 30 seconds, the breaker opens. Subsequent calls fail in microseconds. After 30 seconds, half-open probes test recovery.

Use Resilience4j or equivalent. Or rely on a service mesh (Istio/Linkerd) to implement transparently.

Layer 3: Graceful degradation for recommendation.

When the breaker is open or calls are failing: - Show users a curated default ("Popular items this week") instead of personalized recommendations. - Or show no recommendations and proceed to checkout normally.

The key: recommendation is a nice-to-have, not core checkout flow. Treat it accordingly. Checkout should not fail because recommendation is down.

Implementation discipline:

  • Document the fallback for every dependency. What's the behavior when it's down?
  • Test the failure modes — chaos engineering. Periodically inject failures and verify graceful degradation.
  • Monitor per-dependency: success rate, latency, circuit breaker state. Alerts on each.

The senior framing: coupling is the enemy of resilience. By giving each dependency its own bulkhead, circuit breaker, and fallback, you isolate failure domains. The recommendation service can have a bad day without taking down checkout. The system gracefully degrades to "checkout works, recommendations don't."

Exercise 2. Your team's read-heavy API is at 80% database CPU. The team is debating: bigger DB instance, read replicas, or caching layer. Walk through the analysis.

Step 1: characterize the load.

What's the actual read pattern? - Repeated requests for the same data? → caching is the right answer (high cache hit ratio). - Diverse, low-cardinality queries (millions of unique keys read once)? → caching has limited benefit; replicas help. - Heavy aggregations? → either, depending on whether aggregations are repeated.

Without this analysis, the wrong choice is expensive.

Step 2: rank options by cost and risk.

Option A: Bigger database instance. - Lowest implementation cost. - Linear cost increase (~2x bigger box = ~2x cost). - Doesn't help with eventual write scaling. - Doesn't help with regional latency. - Ceiling: single-machine limits.

Option B: Read replicas. - Moderate implementation cost — replication lag handling, sticky reads (Module 10), read-your-writes mitigations. - Cost scales with replica count. - Doesn't reduce total work — same query load, just distributed. - Helps with regional reads if replicas are placed regionally.

Option C: Caching layer. - Implementation cost depends on which layer (in-process, Redis, CDN). Cache-aside is well-understood. - Cost scales sub-linearly — good cache hit ratios mean dramatic database load reduction. - Cache invalidation complexity (stale data, race conditions). - The biggest performance lever if access patterns are repetitive.

Step 3: combine if appropriate.

Real systems often combine all three: - Bigger database for core capacity. - Read replicas for scaling reads + regional latency. - Caching layer to absorb repetitive load.

These aren't either/or. The question is sequencing.

Recommendation: caching first.

For most read-heavy workloads, caching offers the best ROI. A 90% cache hit ratio means 10x reduction in database load — far more than 2x bigger hardware. Add a Redis layer with cache-aside pattern. Measure database CPU after deployment.

If caching alone isn't enough, then add read replicas. Vertical scaling is rarely the right answer at this point — it's expensive, has limits, and doesn't compound benefits.

The senior reflex: match solution to load shape. Repeated access → cache. Distributed read → replicas. Capacity ceiling → bigger box. Don't pick by what's familiar.

Exercise 3. Explain to non-technical Sam, in two sentences, what backpressure is and why it prevents outages.

Backpressure is a system explicitly telling its callers "I'm full, please slow down" when it can't keep up — instead of accepting more work and crashing under the load. Without backpressure, a slow service silently accumulates queued work until it runs out of memory and crashes, often taking down the services that depend on it; with backpressure, the slow service stays up at reduced capacity and the whole system gracefully slows down together.

There are no dumb questions

Q: What's the difference between a circuit breaker and a retry?

A: They're complementary. A retry attempts the same operation again on failure (with backoff). A circuit breaker stops attempting calls altogether when the downstream is clearly broken. Retries on a broken service amplify load (cascading failure). The pattern: circuit breaker wraps the retry, opening when retries fail too often.

Q: Should I use exponential backoff for retries?

A: Yes, with jitter. Exponential backoff (wait 1s, 2s, 4s, 8s) prevents retries from concentrating in waves. Jitter (small random offset) prevents synchronized retry storms across many clients. Always pair exponential backoff with jitter — the AWS retry libraries do this by default.

Q: How do I set the right circuit breaker threshold?

A: Workload-dependent. Start with: 50% failure rate over 30 seconds opens the breaker, 30 second wait before half-open. Tune by monitoring how often the breaker opens vs how often the downstream is actually broken. Too aggressive = unnecessary opens; too lenient = the breaker doesn't kick in until cascading failure starts.

Q: What about caching dynamic data?

A: Tricky. The shorter the TTL, the lower the hit ratio. For high-write workloads, traditional caching doesn't help much. Consider: read-through caches with explicit invalidation via CDC, or write-through caches that stay coherent. For genuinely write-heavy data, caching often isn't the answer — focus on read replicas or denormalization.

Q: How do service meshes (Istio, Linkerd) fit in?

A: They implement many of these patterns transparently — circuit breakers, retries, timeouts, mTLS, observability — at the network layer. Application code doesn't need to know. Trade-off: simpler app code, more complex infrastructure. Modern Kubernetes-based architectures use service meshes heavily; smaller systems may find them overkill.

Q: What's "chaos engineering"?

A: The practice of deliberately injecting failures into production systems to verify resilience. Netflix's Chaos Monkey randomly kills instances. Litmus and Gremlin are modern tools. The discipline: test the failure modes by triggering them in controlled ways, before they happen in uncontrolled ways. A system isn't resilient until you've verified it's resilient.

Q: Should I have a separate database per service in microservices?

A: Generally yes — it's a key tenet of microservice architecture. Each service owns its data store. Cross-service queries go through APIs, not shared databases. The trade-off: distributed transactions become harder (Module 13's sagas). The discipline is worth it for failure isolation — one service's database problem can't propagate via shared infrastructure.

Q: What's the difference between availability and reliability?

A: Often used interchangeably. Availability = the system is up and responding (% uptime). Reliability = the system behaves correctly when it is up. A buggy system can be highly available (always responding) but unreliable (always wrong). The senior framing: both matter, and they're usually pursued together via the same disciplines.

Three myths to dynamite

"Retries always make systems more reliable."

False. Retries amplify load. A struggling downstream service receives more requests when retries kick in, gets slower, triggers more retries. Without backoff, jitter, and circuit breakers, retries are how cascading failures happen. Retries with proper discipline help; naive retries hurt.

"Five nines is the gold standard."

False. Five nines (99.999%, 5 minutes/year of downtime) is appropriate for telco and financial trading. Most B2C apps target three nines (99.9%, ~9 hours/year). Each additional nine costs roughly 10x more. Match availability targets to business need, not aspiration. Senior engineers question "we need five nines" claims.

"Cache everything for performance."

False. Caching adds operational complexity and consistency risk. For low-traffic data, the cache hit ratio is too low to matter. For frequently-updated data, the invalidation complexity exceeds the value. Cache the hot path, not everything. Profile to find the actual hot spots before adding caching layers.

Interview gotchas

Suggesting "we'll add retries" without backoff and circuit breakers. Retries without discipline cause cascading failures. Senior engineers always pair retries with exponential backoff, jitter, and circuit breaker semantics.

Recommending caching as a default. Cache adds complexity. The right answer is "cache the specific hot paths" — not "cache everything." Identify the actual repetitive reads first.

Treating "high availability" as a checkbox. HA is an architectural discipline, not a feature. The number of nines must match the actual cost-of-downtime. Don't claim five nines without showing the architectural commitment.

Forgetting cache invalidation. "We'll add Redis" is incomplete. The plan must include: how is data updated? What's the TTL? What about race conditions? What happens when the cache is cold? Senior engineers walk through the failure modes.

Recommending multi-region active-active without justification. It's expensive and operationally complex. The legitimate reasons: latency-critical multi-geo presence, regulatory data residency, or extreme availability needs. Don't cargo-cult Netflix.

Mock interview

Walk me through what happens during a cascading failure and how you'd prevent it.

Let me work through a concrete example, then the prevention.

The cascading failure pattern:

Service A calls Service B. B normally responds in 50ms. Under load, B starts taking 5 seconds.

A's threads start blocking on B. A's connection pool to B fills with pending requests. New incoming requests to A get queued internally. Memory grows.

The upstream callers (let's call them user-facing) start timing out their calls to A. They retry. Now A is getting more requests.

Meanwhile, B is getting all of A's blocked threads' retries. B gets slower under more load. The cycle accelerates.

Within a few minutes: - A's memory exhausts; OOM kill. - The user-facing services depending on A start failing. - Their callers retry, hammering them harder. - Total system outage in 10-15 minutes from a single slow downstream.

The prevention has multiple layers, in roughly this order of importance:

Layer 1 — bounded queues with backpressure. A has a bounded queue for outgoing requests to B. When full, A returns 503 to its callers immediately. The callers know to back off. A doesn't accumulate work it can't finish.

Layer 2 — circuit breaker on B. After N consecutive failures (or a high failure rate), the circuit opens. A stops trying to call B at all. Calls fail in microseconds — no waiting. A's resources are preserved. A can return degraded responses to its callers based on the open circuit.

Layer 3 — bulkhead per downstream. A has separate connection pools for each downstream service. When B's pool fills, only B-bound calls block. Calls to C, D, E continue normally. Failure is contained to B's pathway.

Layer 4 — retries with backoff and jitter. When clients retry A, they don't all retry simultaneously. Exponential backoff (1s, 2s, 4s, 8s) with random jitter spreads the retry load. No retry storm.

Layer 5 — graceful degradation. A's response when B is unavailable: return a sensible default, cached data, or a partial response. The user gets something, not a 500. The system stays functional in a reduced state.

Layer 6 — observability. Per-dependency metrics: success rate, latency p99, circuit breaker state. Alerts on each. The on-call engineer sees "B is degrading" within seconds, not after the cascade has spread.

The senior framing: the goal isn't preventing all failures — it's preventing failure propagation. A single slow service should be a paged-but-recoverable incident, not a system-wide outage. The patterns above are the standard discipline.

I'd also mention: most teams don't have all six layers from day one. The progression is to add them as you learn from incidents. But you can plan for them — recognize which layers your system is missing during design.

Six-layer prevention plan. Concrete failure timeline. The closing — "preventing failure propagation" — is the senior reframing of the goal.

Your team is at 99.9% availability and a stakeholder wants 99.99%. What does that conversation look like?

This is one of those "negotiate before commit" conversations.

Step 1: surface the real cost.

99.9% = ~9 hours of downtime per year. 99.99% = ~52 minutes. The 10x reduction has a roughly 10x cost.

What does 10x cost mean architecturally? Usually: - Multi-region redundancy (running everything 2x). - More aggressive observability and alerting (more SREs on call). - Faster automated failover (more sophisticated orchestration). - Comprehensive chaos engineering (testing failure modes regularly). - Higher cloud spend on standby capacity.

I'd estimate the engineering cost upfront — say 2 engineers full-time for 6 months to architect, plus ongoing SRE coverage, plus 1.5-2x cloud costs. Make the cost real before agreeing.

Step 2: question the requirement.

Why 99.99%? Possible answers: - "Our SLA promises it" — verify; SLAs are often aspirational, and customers may accept 99.95% in practice. - "Our competitor offers it" — what does their actual track record show? (Often less than they claim.) - "Customers complain about downtime" — what specifically? Maybe the actual issue is incident duration (we recover slowly when down), not frequency. That's cheaper to fix. - "A regulator requires it" — usually a real constraint, but typically with carve-outs for planned maintenance.

The honest answer might be "we're at 99.9% but our incident duration is too long when we're down." Optimizing recovery is much cheaper than reaching 99.99% headline number.

Step 3: propose the staircase.

Don't go from 99.9% to 99.99% in one project. Stage: - 99.95% (4 hours/year) — usually achievable with better incident response, no architectural change. - 99.97% — better failover automation. - 99.99% — multi-region, full chaos engineering.

Each step has discrete cost and benefit. The team can reassess at each.

Step 4: agree on measurement.

Five nines on the whole system might be aspirational. Five nines on the core checkout flow might be feasible. Define exactly what's measured. The number means nothing without scope.

Step 5: socialize the trade-off.

99.99% means feature velocity drops — every change is more carefully tested, deployed during specific windows, monitored more aggressively. The team will ship slower in exchange for higher availability. Stakeholders need to know that.

The senior framing: availability targets are negotiations, not declarations. The stakeholder said "99.99%" but probably meant "fewer customer-impacting incidents." Match the engineering investment to the actual problem, not the requested number.

If after all this they still want 99.99%, fine — we'll plan and resource it appropriately. But many of these conversations end with "actually, we just want better incident response," which is much cheaper.

Diplomatic and pragmatic. Explores the real motivation behind the request before committing. The "staircase" approach is practical. The closing — "availability targets are negotiations, not declarations" — is senior reframing.

You're designing a service that calls Redis for caching, Postgres for primary data, and an external payment API. Walk me through how you'd handle each dependency's potential failure.

Each dependency has different failure semantics, so each gets a tailored approach.

Redis (cache):

Failure mode: Redis is unreachable, slow, or returns wrong data.

Approach: cache failures should never break the service. The pattern: - Wrap Redis calls in a circuit breaker. - On Redis failure (timeout, connection refused, slow): fall through to Postgres directly. - Log the cache miss; alert on sustained miss rate. - Application logic shouldn't require Redis — it should always work without it, just slower.

Latency: Redis calls are bounded (~5ms timeout). On miss/failure, fall back to Postgres immediately.

Postgres (primary data):

Failure mode: connection failures, replication lag, slow queries, primary down.

Approach: Postgres is a hard dependency for write operations. The pattern: - Connection pool with bounded size (e.g., 50 connections). - Statement timeout configured (e.g., 5 seconds — protect against runaway queries). - For reads: connection to either primary or replica. Tag operations as "must-be-fresh" (primary) or "tolerate-stale" (replicas). - Circuit breaker on the entire database connection pool — if connections fail, the breaker can suspend operations to give the database time to recover.

Failover handling: if the primary fails over (Module 10), the connection pool needs to reconnect. Most pools handle this transparently with health checks.

Graceful degradation: for read operations, if both primary and replicas are unavailable, serve stale cache data with a "data may be stale" indicator. For writes, return errors to the user — there's no graceful path.

External payment API:

Failure mode: timeout, 5xx errors, intermittent failures, slow.

Approach: payment is irreversible and must be handled with care. The pattern: - Idempotency keys (Module 13): every payment attempt has a unique key. Retries with the same key get the cached result. - Aggressive timeouts: don't wait 30 seconds for a payment API to respond. Cap at 5-10 seconds. - Circuit breaker: if the payment API is failing consistently, fail fast. Show users "payment is temporarily unavailable; please try again in a few minutes." - Async processing path: for non-time-critical payments, queue and retry asynchronously. The user sees "your payment is being processed" rather than waiting. - Outbox pattern (Module 13): payment intents are recorded in a database table with status tracking. If the payment service is unreachable, the intent is stored; a worker retries later.

Sensitivity: never silently retry payments without idempotency. Double-charging is a worse failure than failing the request.

Cross-cutting: - All three get observability — per-dependency latency, error rate, circuit state. - All three have on-call alerts. - The service has overall health checks — if any required dependency is down, the service health check reports degraded. - Chaos testing periodically validates the fallback paths.

The senior framing: each dependency has unique failure semantics. Cache failures should be invisible to users; database failures should be honest errors; payment failures should be structured (idempotent retries, async paths, clear user messaging). The pattern is the same — circuit breakers, bounded resources, graceful degradation — but the implementation differs by criticality.

Tailored response per dependency. Specific timeouts and patterns. The cross-cutting observability and chaos testing show real production thinking. The "each dependency has unique failure semantics" framing is the senior pattern.

Last one. The team has decided to add multi-region active-active. What's your reaction?

My first reaction is to ask: why?

Multi-region active-active is one of the most operationally expensive architectures you can adopt. Before I agree, I'd want to understand the actual driver:

Legitimate reasons:

(a) Latency-critical multi-geo presence. Users in EU and APAC need <100ms response times; can't be served from a single region without WAN-level latency. Most apps don't actually need this — modern apps with 100-300ms latency feel fine to most users.

(b) Regulatory data residency. GDPR requires EU data stays in EU; equivalent rules for other jurisdictions. This is the most common legitimate reason for multi-region.

(c) Extreme availability requirements. Single-region multi-AZ delivers ~99.95-99.99%. If you need higher (financial trading, payment infrastructure), multi-region is required.

(d) Disaster recovery (passive). Active-passive multi-region for DR is much simpler than active-active. If the requirement is "survive a regional outage," DR is often the right answer.

Illegitimate reasons:

"Netflix does it." Netflix's traffic justifies it. Most companies don't have Netflix's traffic. "Customers want it." Customers usually want reliability, not specifically multi-region. Single-region with strong DR can deliver that. "It's the modern way." It's one modern way, suited to specific requirements.

If a legitimate reason applies, the cost is real:

  • Conflict resolution: writes from different regions can conflict. The team must explicitly handle this (LWW, CRDTs, app-level merge — Module 10).
  • Data residency complexity: schemas must respect regional rules. Some data is region-locked; some replicates globally.
  • Cross-region partitions: cloud regions occasionally partition. The architecture must work during the partition without diverging.
  • Operational maturity: multi-region requires sophisticated observability, on-call coverage in multiple time zones, and runbooks for region-specific incidents.
  • Cost: roughly 2x infrastructure cost, plus engineering time for ongoing operations.
  • Velocity tax: deploys are more careful. Schema changes coordinate across regions. New features have regional considerations.

My recommendation:

If the driver is (a), (b), or (c) — proceed with eyes open. Plan for 6-12 months of architectural work. Invest in observability and chaos testing.

If the driver is (d), suggest active-passive DR instead. Much cheaper, similar disaster recovery properties.

If the driver is none of the above, push back. The team is committing to a multi-quarter architectural project for marginal benefit. Re-evaluate after collecting actual user feedback or business requirements.

The senior framing: architecture should follow requirements, not aspirations. Multi-region active-active is sometimes the right answer, but it's frequently the wrong one. Ask why before committing.

Pragmatic and somewhat skeptical. Distinguishes legitimate from illegitimate reasons explicitly. Realistic on cost. The "architecture follows requirements, not aspirations" framing is the right level of seniority for this decision.

Cheat sheet

HA, Scaling, Caching & Backpressure — One Page

Availability targets

  • 99% (3.65 days/yr) — internal tools.
  • 99.9% (8.76 hr/yr) — most B2C apps.
  • 99.99% (52 min/yr) — critical SaaS.
  • 99.999% (5 min/yr) — telco, financial trading.
  • Each nine costs ~10x more than the previous.

Cascading failure prevention (the patterns)

  1. Backpressure — bounded queues, reject excess, signal upstream.
  2. Circuit breaker — stop calling broken downstream after N failures.
  3. Bulkhead — separate pools per dependency; isolate failure.
  4. Retries with exponential backoff + jitter — don't synchronize storms.
  5. Graceful degradation — sensible defaults when dependencies fail.
  6. Observability — per-dependency metrics, alerts.

Caching

  • Cache-aside (lazy-load) — most common.
  • Read-through — cache fetches on miss.
  • Write-through — cache writes to DB.
  • Write-behind — cache async-flushes to DB.

Hard problems: cache invalidation, race conditions, thundering herd.

Mitigations: short TTLs, single-flight, stale-while-revalidate, CDC-driven invalidation.

Caching hierarchy

Browser → CDN edge → reverse proxy → app cache → distributed cache → DB query cache → DB buffer pool → DB disk.

Each layer is a chance to skip work.

Vertical vs horizontal scaling

  • Vertical: bigger box. Simple but ceiling.
  • Horizontal: more boxes. Scales but adds coordination.

The senior progression: 1. Single node. 2. Vertical scale. 3. Read replicas. 4. Caching. 5. Horizontal partitioning (Module 15). 6. Multi-region (only when justified).

Multi-region active-active

Justified by: multi-geo latency, regulatory residency, extreme availability. Costs: conflict resolution, data residency, partition handling, operational maturity, ~2x infra spend, velocity tax. Alternative for DR: active-passive — much cheaper, same disaster recovery properties.

Graceful degradation discipline

For every dependency: document what happens when it fails. The answers should be: error, stale data, default, or background queue. Failures are expected events, not exceptions.

Tools by category

  • Circuit breakers: Hystrix (deprecated), Resilience4j, Polly, gobreaker.
  • Service mesh: Istio, Linkerd (handles many patterns transparently).
  • Caching: Redis, Memcached, in-process LRU caches.
  • Adaptive concurrency: Netflix concurrency-limits, AWS adaptive retry.
  • Chaos engineering: Litmus, Gremlin, Chaos Monkey.

Anti-patterns

  • Retries without backoff and jitter.
  • Caching without invalidation strategy.
  • Multi-region without justification.
  • Five-nines aspiration without architectural commitment.
  • "We'll add Redis" without articulating consistency model.

The 30-second verbal answer

"HA isn't a feature — it's a discipline of designing systems that gracefully degrade under load. Backpressure prevents cascading failures by signaling upstream when overloaded. Circuit breakers stop calling broken services. Bulkheads isolate failures to their domain. Caching reduces load on origin. Multi-region active-active is expensive — only justify it with multi-geo latency, regulatory data residency, or extreme availability needs. The senior framing: failures are expected events; design for graceful degradation."
